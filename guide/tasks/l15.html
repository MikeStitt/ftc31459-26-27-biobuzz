L15: one OpMode a driver could take to a match¶

What you will have when this is done¶

One teleop with all of it in. The sticks are shaped. They drive field relative. They hold the heading they were left at. The right bumper means the robot’s own directions. A points the robot at 45 deg while the driver drives. Y hands the robot to the follower until a stick moves.

Before you start¶

  • L13 is finished. It is the last of the pieces this one puts together.

  • task.l8 is done, because this runs the encoder localizer beside the real one.

  • About 3 m by 3 m of clear floor.

You write two lines. Everything else in L15CombinedOpMode.java is given, and every part of it was written in an earlier lesson.

The steps¶

Step 1: run the second localizer again¶

TODO 1 adds your MecanumEncoderLocalizer as a shadow, under the name driveWheelEncoders, exactly as task.l8 did. It is two lines and they are the same two lines.

The name is the whole point of copying it rather than inventing one. Panels draws a series per name, so L8’s plot and this one land on top of each other, and a run of L15 can be compared with a run of L8.

You’ll know it worked when this prints BUILD SUCCESSFUL:

./gradlew :TeamCode:testDebugUnitTest --tests '*LessonsTest.l15*'

If it didn’t, one message covers every way of getting this wrong:

  • the shadow localizer is still running, with no numbers. The test looks for one published key, Localizer/driveWheelEncoders/x_in. It cannot tell a missing localizer from a misspelled name, so check the spelling before you go looking for anything else. Watched, with the name typed as encoders: this same message, and nothing else in the suite noticed.

In Panels you should see six values: x_in, y_in and heading_deg under Localizer/driveWheelEncoders/, and the same three under Localizer/pinPoint/.

Step 2: read what you are about to drive¶

Nothing below is new, and that is the point of the lesson. Read driveTheRobot() before you run it, and find the lesson each part came from.

  • Both drive sticks go through deadband and then squared, from L3. The turn stick is deadbanded only. Squaring the turn as well makes a small correction almost nothing, and steering wants that hundredth.

  • Y hands the robot to the follower, and any stick past the deadband takes it back, from L14.

  • The turn power comes from HeadingHold, from L13. A held stick steers; letting go sets the target.

  • A aims at 45 deg, which is HeadingHold again through a different door. It sets a target the driver did not leave, and translation stays with the driver.

  • The right bumper chooses sticks or fieldRelative, from L11 and L12.

Four things want to turn the robot and they are settled in one fixed order: the driver’s turn stick, then A’s aim, then the held heading, then the follower. Each one only gets a say when the one above it is quiet.

You’ll know it worked when you can name the lesson every line came from. There is no test for that, and it is the check that matters here.

Step 3: drive all four of them¶

Send the code to the robot. Run L15 Combined and press PLAY. Try them in this order: field relative, bumper held, A, Y.

You’ll know it worked when each one does what its lesson did, and switching between them needs nothing but a thumb.

If it didn’t, and one of them misbehaves on its own, go back to that lesson’s page. This page adds no arithmetic, so a fault here is a fault there.

If it didn’t, and A turns the robot but the driving stops while it turns, check that you are reading the given code and not your own. Aiming and driving happen in the same loop, on purpose.

What has not been done here

Step 3 has not been performed on a robot for this page. Watched in the test harness instead, with A pressed and the drive stick held: the robot swings from 0 deg to 48.7 deg in half a second, overshoots 45 deg by about 4 deg, and is inside a degree of it 1.25 s after the press, all while the drive stick keeps its own two powers near 0.5.

What you just did¶

You wrote two lines and got a competition teleop. That is what the last seven lessons were for: every part lives in one place, so putting them together is choosing an order rather than writing code.

The order is the design, and it is worth saying out loud. The driver beats everything. A button beats nothing at all. The follower gets what nobody else has asked for. A teleop built the other way round, where the robot decides and the driver argues with it, is the one that loses matches.
