# L12: give the driver both

## What you will have when this is done

The sticks mean the field, the way L11 left them, until the right bumper is held. While it is held
they mean the robot. Let go and they mean the field again.

## Before you start

- [L11](l11.md) is finished. Both doors this lesson chooses between are already written.
- About 2 m by 2 m of clear floor.

You write seven lines in one file, `L12RobotRelativeButtonOpMode.java`. The three sticks are already
read for you above the blank.

## The steps

### Step 1: choose a door with the bumper

Field relative is the better way to drive most of the time. It is not the better way to line up
against something. Backing straight out of a corner, or nudging along a wall, is easier when forward
means the robot's own forward.

So both are worth having, and an `if` is all it takes. Held bumper: `drivetrain.sticks`, the
robot's own directions. Not held: `drivetrain.fieldRelative`, with the heading first. Publish
which one is in force while you are there.

Held, not toggled. With a hold, letting go always puts the driver back where they started, and their
thumb tells them which mode they are in. With a toggle they have to remember, in a match, with
somebody shouting.

**You'll know it worked when** this prints `BUILD SUCCESSFUL`:

```
./gradlew :TeamCode:testDebugUnitTest --tests '*LessonsTest.l12*'
```

**If it didn't**, open the report gradle names. The test pushes one stick with the robot turned a
quarter turn, so field relative reads 0 at the wheels and robot relative reads 1.

- `robot relative while held expected:<1.0> but was:<0.0>`. The blank is still open. Nothing
  chooses, so nothing drives.
- `field relative by default expected:<0.0> but was:<1.0>`. The two branches are swapped. The
  bumper is the exception, and the `if` should read the bumper, not the lack of it.
- `and back again on release expected:<0.0> but was:<1.0>`. The bumper is flipping a remembered
  flag instead of being read fresh every loop. This is the toggle, and this is the test that
  catches it.

No test watches the log line. It is one call, and Panels is where it earns its place: a driver who
says "it went crooked" and a graph that says which mode was in force settle the question between
them.

### Step 2: drive both ways without stopping

Send the code to the robot. Run `L12 Robot Relative Button` and press PLAY. Drive field relative
for a while. Then turn the robot so its nose points sideways, hold the right bumper, and push the
stick away from you.

**You'll know it worked when** the robot drives along its own nose while you hold the bumper. Let
go, and it goes back to driving away from you.

**If it didn't**, and holding the bumper changes nothing, look at your two branches. The one that
calls `sticks` is the robot relative one.

## What you just did

This is the first lesson that is a driver's decision rather than a robot's. Nothing here is new
arithmetic. Both doors existed already, and what you added is who chooses and when.

That is most of what a competition teleop turns out to be: a small number of ways to drive, and a
gamepad laid out so a driver can reach them without looking.
